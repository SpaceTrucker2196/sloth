# RFC 8996 — Deprecating TLS 1.0 and TLS 1.1

TLS 1.0 (1999) and TLS 1.1 (2006) are formally deprecated. RFC 8996
makes negotiating them a MUST NOT, on the grounds that both mandate
cipher suites and constructions — SHA-1 in the signature and MAC,
CBC modes with known padding weaknesses, no AEAD requirement — that no
longer meet the bar.

The deprecation is unusual in being *unconditional*: not "prefer
something better", but "do not negotiate these".

## Why sloth cites this

`ALERT_TYPE_WEAK_TLS` reports a negotiated version or cipher suite that
this document deprecates, read from the ClientHello and ServerHello,
which are in the clear by necessity.

Mapped to T1600 (Weaken Encryption) rather than to an interception
technique, because what sloth observes is the *weakened state*, not an
adversary exploiting it. The weakness is real whether or not anyone is
on the path.

Sloth also computes a JA3 fingerprint from the same handshake fields —
see `docs/wiki/ja3-fingerprinting.md`. That is a separate question
(which client is this?) asked of the same bytes.

## What it does not say

**A deprecated version is not a broken one.** Attacks on TLS 1.0 in
practice need conditions — a position on the path, many sessions, a
cooperative client — that a passive alert cannot assume. This is a
posture finding, and its answer is a migration plan.

**Embedded devices are the common trigger.** Building-management
controllers, medical devices and industrial gear routinely speak TLS 1.0
and cannot be updated. The finding stands; the remediation is
segmentation rather than an upgrade.

**A strong version can still carry a weak suite**, and RFC 8996 is only
about the version. Sloth reads both, and the cipher-suite half is where
a TLS 1.2 connection can still be worth reporting.
