# CVE-2023-52424 — SSID Confusion

**The SSID is not bound into key derivation.** The 4-way handshake
authenticates the network's credentials, not its name, so a client that
believes it is connected to one network can be connected to another that
shares the credential — or to a rogue AP advertising a trusted name
while offering weaker protection.

The consequence is that a network's *name* carries none of the trust
users and clients place in it.

## Why sloth cites this

Three alerts share this basis, and the spread is the point: the same
design gap produces attacks that look different on the air.

**`SSID_CONFUSION`** is the direct observable — the same SSID appearing
on a second BSSID with a **weaker RSN posture** than the strongest
posture sloth has seen for that name. A legitimate multi-VAP controller
does not downgrade its own security across BSSIDs for one SSID, which is
what makes this high-signal rather than merely suspicious.

**`EVIL_TWIN`** is the coarser form: same name, mismatched BSSID or
cipher.

**`KARMA_AP`** is the inverse — one BSSID answering to *many* names,
which works for the same reason: no name is bound to anything.

## What it does not say

**It does not make every same-SSID pair an attack.** Roaming, mesh and
multi-band deployments produce same-SSID different-BSSID pairs
constantly, which is why `EVIL_TWIN` needs a *mismatch* — in cipher,
vendor OUI, or RSN posture — rather than duplication alone. Issue #51
was exactly this false positive: a cross-vendor range extender reported
as a rogue.

**It does not require an adversary for the downgrade to exist.** An AP
in WPA2/WPA3 transition mode offers the weak lane by configuration. That
is `WPA_DOWNGRADE`'s finding, and it is a posture problem rather than an
intrusion.

**Sloth cannot see which network a client believes it is on.** That
belief lives in the supplicant. What sloth reports is that the
conditions for the confusion are present on the air.
