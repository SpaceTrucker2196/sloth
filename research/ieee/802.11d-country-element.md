# IEEE 802.11d — the Country element, and what its absence means

Clause-annotated summary. The standard text is copyrighted; this is our
own description of the mechanism sloth reads, not an excerpt.

## What 802.11d added

Before 802.11d a station had to be built or configured for the country
it would be used in: which channels it may use and at what power was
fixed in firmware. 802.11d ("regulatory domain extensions", 2001, since
folded into the base standard) moved that knowledge onto the air. The
AP names its regulatory domain in the **Country element** (tag 7) of
its Beacon and Probe Response frames — a two-letter ISO 3166-1 code, an
environment octet, and either subband triplets (first channel, number
of channels, maximum transmit power) or operating triplets — and a
multi-domain-capable station configures itself from it.

The element is present in a beacon whenever
`dot11MultiDomainCapabilityActivated` or
`dot11SpectrumManagementRequired` is true (§9.3.3.2 beacon frame body;
§9.4.2.8). The second is the important one for 5 GHz: §11.8 spectrum
management — radar detection (DFS) and transmit power control — is
required by the regulators of almost every domain on the 5 GHz
channels where radar operates, and an AP doing spectrum management
names its domain.

`src/reg_ie.c` parses the element (#101 slice 1) and `beacon_ap_t.reg`
retains it per BSSID (slice 2).

## Why absence on 5 GHz is a signal

A 5 GHz AP that has never sent a Country element is telling its
stations nothing about where it is. The common causes are a build that
switched 802.11d off — hostapd's `ieee80211d=0`, which is its default, needing a
`country_code` as well to turn on — and the stripped OpenWrt images rogue
kits are assembled from. That is why the issue calls absence the cheap,
high-specificity tell for a lab-built rogue: commercial and consumer
5 GHz equipment ships with a domain configured, because without one it
may not use the DFS channels at all.

## What absence does not prove

The detector's honesty depends on these, and its alert text carries the
first one verbatim:

1. **No advisory exists.** There is no CVE, CERT note or vendor advisory
   for a missing Country element. `ALERT_TYPE_COUNTRY_ABSENT` is a
   heuristic built on this clause, labelled as one, and reports a
   non-compliant configuration — never an attacker. Its ATT&CK column
   is empty for that reason, and
   `tests/test_research_corpus.c :: test_uncitable_kinds_with_a_real_basis_are_still_cited`
   holds this document as its source.
2. **Pre-802.11d gear is exempt.** Equipment built before multi-domain
   operation existed never sent the element. sloth suppresses the
   finding for `phy == "legacy"`, as the TPC rule does.
3. **Missing is not the same as not heard.** A channel-hopping radio
   misses most beacons. The retained envelope is sticky per element, so
   one beacon carrying a Country element settles it for the session,
   and the rule requires a floor of both elapsed time and beacon count
   before it reads silence as absence.
4. **A malformed element is not an absent one.** The AP tried; the
   detail says how many malformed elements were seen.
5. **6 GHz is out of scope here.** The 6 GHz regulatory envelope rides
   other elements (HE Operation's 6 GHz Operation Information and the
   Transmit Power Envelope) and is a separate slice of #101.

## Related sloth alert kinds

`ALERT_TYPE_COUNTRY_ABSENT` — LOW for any 5 GHz AP in range, WARN when
the BSSID is designated or the SSID is designated from a BSSID that is
not. Sibling of `ALERT_TYPE_TPC_ABSENT_HIGH_BAND`
(`research/ieee/802.11-2020-11.8-spectrum-management.md`), which is
about the power ceiling rather than the domain and stays
ownership-gated.
