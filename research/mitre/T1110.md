# T1110 — Brute Force

An adversary without valid credentials obtains them by trying many.
ATT&CK splits this by *shape*: **.001 Password Guessing** is many
passwords against one account, **.003 Password Spraying** is one
password against many accounts — deliberately, because spraying stays
under per-account lockout thresholds that guessing trips.

That distinction is the whole reason sloth counts what it counts.

## Why sloth cites this

Five alerts share this technique, and all five detect the same thing at
different layers: **repeated authentication failures from one source
inside a window**.

| alert | protocol | what a failure looks like on the wire |
|---|---|---|
| `SSH_BRUTE_FORCE` | SSH | connection closed without reaching the session stage |
| `RDP_BRUTE_FORCE` | RDP | repeated connection attempts to 3389 |
| `SNMP_COMMUNITY_BRUTE` | SNMP | varying community strings against one agent |
| `MQTT_BROKER_BRUTE` | MQTT | CONNACK with a non-zero return code |
| `KERB_PREAUTH_BURST` | Kerberos | AS-REQ pre-authentication failures |

`KERB_PREAUTH_BURST` maps to **.003** rather than .001, and that is not
cosmetic. A burst of Kerberos pre-authentication failures across
*different principals* is spraying — the shape chosen specifically to
avoid the lockout a per-account attack would trigger. Counting failures
without regard to how many accounts they span would call spraying and
guessing the same thing and miss the one that evades the defence.

## What it does not say

**It does not say a failure is an attack.** A misconfigured backup job
with a stale password produces exactly this signature, indefinitely, and
it is the single most common benign trigger for all five rules. The
thresholds exist to separate a rate no human reaches from one an
automation does; they cannot separate a hostile automation from a broken
one.

**It does not license alerting on a single failure.** Every one of these
rules is a rate over a window, and the window matters as much as the
count: a service restarting and re-authenticating produces a burst that
is not sustained.

**It says nothing about success.** Sloth sees the failures. A brute
force that succeeds on its first attempt produces no alert at all, which
is worth remembering when reading a quiet screen.
