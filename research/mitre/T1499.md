# T1499 — Endpoint Denial of Service

Not the link, the *endpoint*. Exhausting a finite resource inside one
machine — a table, a state machine, a parser — rather than the capacity
of the network between them.

The distinction matters operationally: an endpoint DoS can be quiet.
It does not need volume if the resource it exhausts is small.

## Why sloth cites this

**`AUTH_FLOOD`** — authentication requests filling an AP's association
table. The table is a few hundred entries; the flood does not have to
saturate anything else.

**`MGMT_FUZZ`** — malformed management frames. The target is the
parser, not a table, and a single frame can be enough. Sloth reports
the malformed-IE shapes it can recognise rather than claiming a crash it
cannot observe.

**`BGP_NOTIFICATION_BURST`** — a BGP session repeatedly reset. Each
NOTIFICATION tears the session down and forces a full re-convergence,
and a peer that keeps sending them keeps a router doing expensive work.

**`SA_QUERY_FLOOD`** (#76) — the MFP-era shape, and the one that is
mostly a *symptom*: the AP is answering spoofed disassociations. What it
costs is the queried station's airtime and, if the exchange fails, its
association.

**`BLOCKACK_ATTACK`** — the quietest of all. A spoofed Block-Ack
Request advances a peer's receive window past frames still in flight;
everything skipped is discarded. No flood, no deauth, nothing a rate
detector sees. The operator's symptom is a client that stopped working.

## What it does not say

**Sloth sees the attempt, never the effect.** Whether the endpoint
actually exhausted anything is a fact inside another machine. Every
alert here says "these frames were transmitted", and on a hardened stack
the same frames produce nothing at all.

**A burst is not necessarily hostile.** A flapping BGP peer with a real
link problem produces `BGP_NOTIFICATION_BURST` honestly, and a
misbehaving driver produces malformed management frames without anyone
intending it. These are the benign triggers, and they are common.
