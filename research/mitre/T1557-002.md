# T1557.002 — ARP cache poisoning

ARP has no authentication. A host asks who owns an IP address and
believes whatever answers — including an unsolicited answer nobody
asked for. An adversary claiming the gateway's address receives the
segment's traffic.

The attack is as old as the protocol and remains effective because the
fix requires switch-side support (dynamic ARP inspection) that most
networks do not deploy.

## Why sloth cites this

`ALERT_TYPE_ARP_SPOOF` fires on the observable: **one IP address
claimed by two different MAC addresses**, or a MAC binding that changes
without the machine having gone away.

Sloth is well placed for this specifically because ARP is broadcast. The
poisoning frames go to every host on the segment by design, so a passive
listener sees the attack itself rather than its consequences — which is
unusual, and worth saying, because most of sloth's L2 detectors see only
what an attack leaves behind.

## What it does not say

**A changed binding is not always an attack.** These are the benign
triggers, and they are frequent:

- **DHCP reassignment.** A lease expires, the address goes to another
  machine, and the binding legitimately changes.
- **Failover and virtual addresses.** VRRP, HSRP and keepalived move an
  IP between physical MACs deliberately — that *is* the feature.
- **A laptop switching between wired and wireless.** Two interfaces, one
  address, in quick succession.

**Gratuitous ARP is normal.** Hosts announce themselves on boot and
after an address change. It is the *conflict* that matters, not the
unsolicited announcement.

**Seeing the poisoning does not mean it worked.** Whether a victim's
cache accepted the entry is a fact inside that victim. A host with a
static ARP entry for its gateway is unaffected and sloth cannot tell.
