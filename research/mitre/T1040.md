# T1040 — Network Sniffing

An adversary on the path reads traffic that was never protected. The
technique needs no exploit; it needs only position, which on a shared
wireless segment or behind a rogue AP is the easy part.

## Why sloth cites this

`ALERT_TYPE_CLEARTEXT_CRED` reports credentials sloth observed in
transit without encryption — FTP `USER`/`PASS`, Telnet, POP3, IMAP,
SMTP AUTH, HTTP Basic.

There is an uncomfortable symmetry here that is worth naming: **sloth is
demonstrating the technique in order to report it.** A passive monitor
that can see a password is proof that anything else on that segment can
too, which is precisely the finding.

That is also why sloth stores what it does. The alert records *that*
credentials were exposed, the protocol, and the endpoints — the evidence
an operator needs to act — and `docs/wiki/jsonl-schema.md` and the
SQLite guardrail tests exist to keep the credential material itself out
of the file. A forensic record that leaks what it is reporting on has
made the problem worse.

## What it does not say

**It does not mean an adversary was present.** This alert fires on a
network where nobody is attacking anything; the credential was exposed
regardless. That is the point — it is a *posture* finding first and an
incident finding only if someone was listening.

**It does not cover what sloth cannot see.** Credentials inside TLS are
invisible, correctly. An empty screen means no cleartext credential was
observed on the traffic sloth captured, not that none was sent.

**Legacy protocols on a management VLAN are the common trigger.** A
switch or printer speaking Telnet to a jump host is the ordinary cause,
and it is still a real finding — just one whose answer is a migration
plan rather than an incident response.
