## What the rule checks

*This section describes the rule as assessed, before #173. What #173 changed (the detail, and how often the rule fires) is in the **Since #173** paragraph at the end of Assessment; file:line references below are to the assessed tree except `rule_file`.*

`rule_wpa_downgrade()` (src/alerts/rules_twin.c:847-905) fires on an AP advertising a lane weaker than its primary one. Nothing here is an attack - every condition is a config the AP broadcasts about itself (the rule's design comment is not beside the function: it sits in src/alerts/rules_wifi_mgmt.c:279-294, "WPA / PMF downgrade posture (#62)").

- **Derivation.** `wifi_downgrade_update()` (src/wifi_assess.c:57-80) runs at the top of `alerts_update()` (src/alerts/engine.c:935) and sets `beacon_ap_t.downgrade_flags` per AP, so the rule and the `[b]` view read one field. `wifi_downgrade_flags()` (wifi_assess.c:17) computes the bits; OWE transition additionally requires finding the paired open companion BSS by SSID (wifi_assess.c:68-78).
- **Observation floor.** Skip APs with `now - first_seen < WPA_DOWNGRADE_MIN_OBSERVED_S` (30 s, include/sloth.h:2465; rules_twin.c:854-855). A single beacon caught mid-`--hop` is a sample, not a configuration.
- **Four kinds** (include/sloth.h:2456-2459), one alert each (loop at rules_twin.c:886):
  - `WPA_DG_TRANSITION_SAE_PSK` (0x1) - PSK and SAE both on offer (WPA2/WPA3 transition).
  - `WPA_DG_OWE_TRANSITION` (0x2) - an OWE BSS paired with an open companion.
  - `WPA_DG_MFP_OPTIONAL` (0x4) - SAE with MFP capable-but-not-required (the Dragonblood primitive).
  - `WPA_DG_WPA1_ALONGSIDE` (0x8) - a legacy WPA1 IE beside the RSN IE (TKIP still on offer).
- **Key.** `wpadg:<bssid>:<kind>` (rules_twin.c:892) - one incident per (BSSID, kind), so an AP offering two weak lanes is two findings "and collapsing them would hide whichever the operator did not read".
- **Exercised escalation.** Scans `s->assoc_reqs[]` for a downgrade-flagged request against this BSSID within the hour (`now - r->ts > 3600` skips, rules_twin.c:870-878); if a client actually took the weak lane, that is `exercised` (#60).
- **Severity.** CRIT when `mine || exercised`, else WARN (rules_twin.c:901).
- **Count.** Plain `fire()`, no evidence IDs, no `match_ip`. Each poll with the posture still standing increments `count`; the detail carries `(observed <N>s)` (format `%lds`, rules_twin.c:894) whose integer moves once a second. `observations` is bumped when the new detail signature differs from the stored `detail_sig` (engine.c:487-488), and that stored copy is refreshed only on an emitted event: a severity change (engine.c:519) or a detail change once the 60 s update floor has passed (`ALERT_UPDATE_MIN_MS`, engine.c:528-534). Between those events the stored copy is stale, so every tick after the seconds value first moves counts as an observation: `observations` follows `evaluations` at the tick rate (4/s or more), not 1/s. The same floor caps emitted detail-updates at one per 60 s.

## The attack

T1600, Weaken Encryption, is "compromise a network device's encryption capability in order to bypass encryption that would otherwise protect data" [2]. sloth maps WPA_DOWNGRADE here because the AP is the device offering the weaker lane - but the rule fires on the *precondition*, not an exploit (docs/views/alerts.md:203 "None is an attack - each is the prerequisite").

The dangerous case is WPA2/WPA3 transition mode. To support pre-WPA3 clients, "a network can operate in a transition mode where WPA2 and WPA3 are simultaneously supported using the same password. In this mode the AP advertises Management Frame Protection (MFP) as optional" [3 §2.3]. Vanhoef & Ronen's Dragonblood paper demonstrates the attack: "we demonstrate a downgrade and dictionary attack against WPA3 when it is operating in transition mode" [3, abstract]. The adversary broadcasts a WPA2-only clone of the SSID; the client connects via WPA2, and "the adversary can forge the first message of the 4-way handshake, since this message is not authenticated"; the client answers with message 2 and "by that point an adversary has captured enough data to perform a dictionary attack" [3 §4.1.1]. The paper is careful that WPA2's own 4-way handshake detects a beacon-forgery downgrade via the authenticated RSNE, so "WPA3 provides forward secrecy, even when using the transition mode of WPA3-SAE" [3 §4.1.1] - the practical exposure is the offline dictionary attack on the captured message 2, and the implementation-specific downgrades that worked even against some WPA3-only clients (Table 2) [3].

The other kinds are related prerequisites: MFP capable-but-not-required is the Dragonblood transition-mode primitive - "the only requirement placed on WPA3 clients is that they must use MFP, even though it is advertised as optional" [3 §2.3], and hostapd documents `ieee80211w` as 0 disabled / 1 optional / 2 required, with `ieee80211w=2` in its WPA3-Personal examples [6]; WPA1/TKIP alongside RSN keeps a cipher the industry deprecated years ago on offer; OWE transition pairs an encrypted-but-unauthenticated BSS with a fully open companion (RFC 8110 §6 describes the companion-BSS deployment) [7].

The mitigation the standard provides is the Transition Disable KDE: once "sufficiently large number of APs in the ESS have been updated to support the more secure alternative", the AP can tell stations to "disable transition mode and less secure security options. This includes use of WEP, TKIP (including use of TKIP as the group cipher), and connections without PMF" [6, `transition_disable`].

## Evidence that proves it

What sloth records:

- The alert detail: `<bssid> "<ssid>" posture=<label> (observed <N>s)` plus `- a client took the weak lane` (exercised) and `- YOUR network` (mine). Labels from `wifi_downgrade_label()`: "WPA2+WPA3 transition", "OWE transition", "MFP optional", "WPA1 alongside RSN" (wifi_assess.c:47-55).
- JSONL `beacon` records carry `downgrade_flags` (WPA_DG_* bits), `akm_bits`, `mfp` (0/1/2), `pairwise`, `group` (docs/wiki/jsonl-schema.md:700) - the AP's advertised posture, straight from the RSNE.
- JSONL `wifi_assoc_req` records carry `downgrade_flags` (ASSOC_DG_*), `prev_akm_bits`, `prev_mfp` - the client side, which is what `exercised` reads (src/assoc_track.c the ASSOC_DG_* derivation).
- `--report` emits a "Downgrade lanes advertised" table and an "Association requests" downgrade table (src/posture.c:218, 261).
- No pcap / no evidence-ring citation: the finding is a standing RSNE posture, not a frame burst (not in the citing-rules list, docs/wiki/pcap-export.md).
- Note: slothweb withholds this type's detail because it carries the SSID (docs/wiki/alert-remediation.md:887-899).

To judge severity: `- a client took the weak lane` (exercised) is the line that turns "offered" into "used" - a `wifi_assoc_req` with `ASSOC_DG_AKM` (SAE dropped for PSK), `ASSOC_DG_MFP` (protection reduced), or `ASSOC_DG_PAIRWISE` (TKIP newly on offer) against this BSSID. `- YOUR network` means it is yours to fix. Correlate with `SSID_CONFUSION` (the AP-side confusion precondition, CVE-2023-52424) and `EVIL_TWIN`/`SAE_PSK_SPLIT` on the same name - a downgrade posture plus a rogue clone is the Dragonblood setup.

What refutes an attack reading entirely: there is no attack to refute - the posture is a true fact. What refutes *urgency* is that the AP is yours and is in a deliberate, documented, time-boxed migration window (the one benign cause, docs/views/alerts.md:203).

## Benign causes

- **A deliberate WPA2/WPA3 migration window** - the intended coexistence config while some clients are still WPA2-only; legitimate but should be short and documented (docs/wiki/alert-remediation.md:887-909). This is the overwhelming majority of trips.
- **MFP-optional by design on a WPA2 network** - the common WPA2 posture; only notable on a SAE BSS.
- **OWE transition** deployed exactly as RFC 8110 §6 prescribes (an open companion BSS during migration to Enhanced Open) [7].
- **A dual-band or multi-VAP unit** configured for broad compatibility.

None of these is an intrusion; all are postures the operator may or may not still need.

## Assessment

**Severity.** WARN for offered-not-exercised, CRIT for `--my-bssid` or exercised, is exactly right. A prerequisite config is a WARN; it earns CRIT only when it is the operator's own AP (theirs to fix now) or when a client has demonstrably been moved onto the weak lane.

**Prevalence.** Transition mode is near-universal during the WPA3 migration [3 §2.3][6]; the Dragonblood attack that weaponises it is real and demonstrated [3] but needs an active rogue WPA2 clone and an offline cracking step. So the base rate of the *posture* is high and the base rate of an *active exploit* behind any given alert is low.

**Reference host noise verdict.** Very noisy by fire count: 672,230,373 fires on 794 keys, max_sev WARN. This is an artifact of per-tick counting of a standing posture, not 672M discrete events. 794 keys ~= 794 distinct (BSSID, kind) pairs = a dense RF neighbourhood full of transition-mode APs; ~846k fires/key at >= 4 polls/s is at most ~59 hours of continuous advertisement per key (an upper bound), i.e. the capture simply ran a long time next to many transition-mode APs. The JSONL stream is **not** quiet for this type. The detail embeds `(observed %lds)` (rules_twin.c:893-899), which changes every second, so the detail signature (a plain FNV-1a over the detail string, engine.c:74-76) changes every second and `fire_inv()` emits an `alert.update` each time the 60 s floor passes (`ALERT_UPDATE_MIN_MS` = `ALERT_UPDATE_MIN_S` x 1000, include/sloth.h:867/874; engine.c:527-537). Every standing key therefore writes one update per minute indefinitely - hundreds of records a minute if hundreds of keys are live at once. This is exactly the "change-only emission must bucket its continuous values" failure agents/AGENTS.md names; bucketing or removing the observed-seconds from the detail would fix it. A second, conditional mechanism: the engine holds `MAX_ALERTS` = 128 incidents (include/sloth.h:823) against up to `MAX_BEACON_APS` = 256 APs x 4 kinds. If more WPA_DOWNGRADE keys are live than slots, `evict_oldest()` (engine.c:346-395) resolves an incident of the most-held type - this one - with reason "evicted", and its key re-creates next poll (new `alert` + `alert.create`, and a wake that shortens the poll wait), so the over-capacity keys churn every tick. Whether the reference host reached that state is not recoverable from the totals given (794 keys over the whole capture, concurrency unknown). RECOMMENDATION: any UI or billing that surfaces this type should read the incident lifecycle, not `count` or `observations` (both are per-second-or-faster for this type); and because almost every key is a benign transition posture, the alert page should lead with the posture label and the exercised/mine flags rather than the raw count. No threshold change is available (the 30 s floor is the only knob and it is compile-time), nor is one warranted - the rule is correctly reporting true facts; the fix is presentation.

**Is the evidence sufficient to act on?** Yes for the posture claim (the RSNE is in the `beacon` record), and yes for the exercised/mine escalations (the `wifi_assoc_req` and ownership inputs). It does not, and does not claim to, prove an attacker is present.

**First step for a responder.** Filter to `- YOUR network` and `- a client took the weak lane`; those are the ones worth action. For an owned AP in transition mode with no current WPA2 clients, move to SAE-only with MFP required (hostapd `wpa_key_mgmt=SAE`, `ieee80211w=2`) and consider the Transition Disable KDE [6]. A neighbour's transition-mode AP is its owner's to change.

**Since #173.** The observed-seconds are bucketed: the detail reads `(observed 30s+)`, then `5m+`, `1h+`, `1d+`, so a standing key's detail changes at most three times in its life and change-only emission holds. `first_seen` on the beacon row still carries the exact age. The over-capacity eviction churn described above is untouched. Test: `tests/test_alert_tick_rate.c`.
