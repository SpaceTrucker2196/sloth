## What the rule checks

`rule_btm_abuse()` (src/alerts/rules_wifi_mgmt.c:199-276) detects an AP pushing a client off with 802.11v instead of deauth.

- **Input.** `btm_forcing_pair(now, window_s, thresh, &bssid, &sta, &total)` (src/action_snoop.c:890-926) walks `BTM_EVT_RING` (256), scores each (BSSID, STA) pair once from its first in-window slot, counts frames carrying Disassociation Imminent (`imminent`) and the total, and returns the pair with the most imminent ones. Events enter via `btm_observe()` (action_snoop.c:842-846), which sets `imminent = (request_mode & BTM_REQ_DISASSOC_IMM)`; the bit is **B2 = 0x04** (src/action_snoop.h:66) per IEEE 802.11-2020 §9.6.14 Figure 9-924 [6]. As with ASSOC_FLOOD, only the single worst pair is returned, so at most one `btm:` key is asserted per tick.
- **Thresholds** (registry, src/tune.c:193-194): `btm.abuse_thresh` default 4 (include/sloth.h:1232), `btm.abuse_window_s` default 60 (:1233).
- **Gate.** Fire only if `imminent >= thresh` (line 205). A Request without B2 leaves the client free to decline (ordinary load balancing) and does not count toward the gate - rate alone would fire on every busy enterprise AP (docs/wiki/btm-abuse.md "The threshold").
- **Key.** `btm:<bssid>-><sta>` (line 253) - per pair. An AP steering many clients is load balancing; one client four times a minute is "not taking no for an answer" (btm-abuse.md).
- **Candidate analysis.** `btm_find()` returns the latest Request's candidate list; the rule flags `twin_target` (a candidate is the rogue half of a detected twin pair, via `btm_candidate_is_twin`) and `fabricated` (a candidate never heard beaconing). Two tells go in the detail: `source never beaconed` and `candidate never beaconed` - both **reported, not escalated on** (computed at lines 222-228 and 249, rendered at 260-261), because on a hopping sensor "never beaconed" means "not while we were listening".
- **Severity.** CRIT when `twin_target || mine || rostered`, else WARN (lines 273-275).
- **Evidence.** `fire_ev()` with `btm_forcing_evidence()` (action_snoop.c:928-944): only the Disassociation-Imminent Requests the threshold counted - an ordinary steer is not cited (docs/wiki/pcap-export.md:152).

## The attack

802.11v BSS Transition Management lets an AP tell a client to move to another BSS. It was added for load balancing and band steering and is polite by design - the AP suggests, the client decides - except for the Disassociation Imminent bit (B2 of the Request Mode field, §9.6.14 Figure 9-924 [6]). With B2 set, the message becomes "you are about to be dropped, here is where to go", and clients honour it [1][6].

That turns a management feature into a forced roam with properties worse than a deauth flood, as Louca, Peratikou & Stavrou demonstrate in "A novel Evil Twin MiTM attack through 802.11v protocol exploitation" [3] (the repo's wiki attributes the same article, by its ScienceDirect PII, to "Ali & Kulkarni (2023)"; Crossref resolves that PII to the Louca paper - see Verification notes): (1) **no proximity requirement** - a BTM Request names the destination BSSID, so the attacker need not out-signal the real AP; (2) **it works on clients that ignore deauth** - IoT/embedded stacks that treat unsolicited deauth as noise still implement 802.11v because roaming support is a certification checkbox; (3) **it looks like management traffic** because it is. `DEAUTH_FLOOD` never sees it - no deauth frame is sent (docs/wiki/btm-abuse.md "The gap this closes").

Tooling: the mechanism is trivially reachable with standard AP software - hostapd's control interface has a `bss_tm_req` command ("send BSS Transition Management Request") whose arguments carry the Disassociation Imminent flag, the disassociation timer and the candidate list [4] - so any attacker running a rogue hostapd can emit it. The same command is what legitimate controllers use for steering, which is the whole false-positive problem.

The abuse maps to **T1498** (Network DoS), not T1557, deliberately: what sloth observes is a client forced off its AP; whether it then lands on an attacker's AP is a separate observation, and "tagging the forcing as AiTM would claim the second half of the attack on the evidence of the first" [1b][6]. When a candidate is a known twin, the `twin_target` escalation records that the landing site is identified, which is the strongest in-rule evidence that this is AiTM setup rather than pure denial of service.

## Evidence that proves it

What sloth records:

- The alert detail: `BTM force: <bssid> -> <sta> <imminent>/<total> req <window>s, candidates=<list>, evil-twin=yes|no` plus `source never beaconed` / `candidate never beaconed` / `- YOUR network` / `- rostered client`.
- Cited evidence frames: the Disassociation-Imminent BTM Requests the threshold counted (docs/wiki/pcap-export.md:152); with `--collect-handshakes` + `--eapol-dir` they are written to the per-alert evidence pcap.
- JSONL `btm_request` records (docs/wiki/jsonl-schema.md:714), one per pair: `req_count`, `imminent_count` ("the field to key on"), `request_mode`, `disassoc_timer` (beacon intervals), `validity_interval`, `candidate_count`, `candidate_0..n`.
- SQLite `btm_requests` in the 12x finding-retention tier, counters upserted with MAX so eviction cannot erase steering a live alert depends on (docs/wiki/btm-abuse.md "Export").
- The `[a]` Deauth view's BTM table: Reqs, Force, Timer, Candidate columns (docs/views/deauth.md:184-207).

To call it an attack: a candidate BSSID that is the rogue half of a detected twin (`evil-twin=yes`), or a candidate/source that never beaconed on a non-hopping capture, or the forcing aimed at a `--my-bssid` network or `--known-macs` client. The cited frames should show B2 set, a short disassoc timer, and a candidate list pointing at a BSSID the operator does not run.

What refutes it: `evil-twin=no`, a candidate that is a known AP beaconing the same SSID (recovery, not attack - docs/views/deauth.md), and a `btm_request` row where the **total** is high but the **imminent** fraction is modest (a stubborn client being asked repeatedly, declining each time). On a `--hop` capture the "never beaconed" tells carry no weight.

## Benign causes

- **Aggressive band steering** - a controller moving a dual-band client between 2.4 and 5 GHz repeatedly because the client keeps coming back; some vendors set Disassociation Imminent to make it stick (docs/wiki/btm-abuse.md). hostapd exposes the frame as a control-interface command, and OpenWrt's usteer requires 802.11v BSS Transition (`option bss_transition '1'`) enabled on every AP it steers between [4][5].
- **A radio going down for maintenance** - a legitimate single Disassociation Imminent per client (the BSS Termination fields exist for this); it usually spreads across many pairs rather than concentrating on one, which per-pair keying exploits.
- **A client that will not leave** - high total, low imminent; the Reqs-vs-Force columns separate this from an attack.

## Assessment

**Severity.** Agree. WARN by default; CRIT when the forcing has an identified rogue landing site (`twin_target`), or hits the operator's own network/device. That is the correct line: a forced roam toward a known twin is AiTM setup, a forced roam toward nowhere-known is denial of service, and both deserve attention but only the first is a completed-chain CRIT on RF evidence alone.

**Prevalence.** The *mechanism* is everywhere - every 802.11v controller steers with it [4][5]. The *abuse* is documented academically [3] but uncommon in the wild versus deauth, because the attacker must emit well-formed 802.11v frames and (for AiTM) stand up a convincing destination. The value of the alert is precisely that it catches the technique deauth detectors miss.

**Reference host noise verdict.** Not seen (no keys, no fires) - expected. Four Disassociation-Imminent steers at one pair in 60 s does not happen absent either a misconfigured aggressive controller or an attacker; per-pair keying stops a busy steering AP from aggregating into a single loud incident. If a controller-managed site did make it noisy, the count is per-tick (one increment per ~250 ms poll while the pair stays over threshold), so a standing steering loop inflates `count` while the detail is stable; the remedy is `--tune btm.abuse_thresh` up or `btm.abuse_window_s` adjustments, and checking whether `evil-twin=no` on all of them (benign steering).

**Is the evidence sufficient to act on?** For the CRIT cases (twin candidate / owned network / rostered client), yes - the cited frames plus the `btm_request` candidate list are a strong basis. For a bare WARN with `evil-twin=no`, it is a prompt to check whether the candidate is your own AP before doing anything.

**First step for a responder.** Read `evil-twin=` and the candidate list. A twin candidate or a candidate BSSID you do not operate means a rogue is steering your client - treat the destination as hostile. A known-AP candidate on the same SSID is your own network steering, and the `btm_request` total-vs-imminent ratio tells you whether it is aggressive-but-legitimate.
