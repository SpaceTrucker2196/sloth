## What the rule checks

Inputs. The control-frame source table in src/ctrl_frames.c, fed by `ctrl_observe()` / `ctrl_observe_frame()` from the monitor path. RTS is control subtype 11 (`CTRL_SUB_RTS`, src/ctrl_frames.h:40). An RTS carries a transmitter address (addr2) and a Duration/ID field; the per-source table keeps, per TA, the RTS count (`sub[CTRL_SUB_RTS]`), `max_nav`, and `first_seen`/`last_seen`. Those last three are **not** RTS-only: `ctrl_observe_frame()` (src/ctrl_frames.c:98-132) updates them for every control frame `ctrl_ta()` attributes to a transmitter - RTS, Block Ack Request, Block Ack and PS-Poll (ctrl_frames.c:78-88). `max_nav` is the raw Duration/ID octets 2-3 of any of those, and a PS-Poll's Duration/ID field is not a duration at all but the station's AID with bits 14-15 set (>= 0xC000 = 49152), so one PS-Poll from a TA puts its `max_nav` above 32767. CTS and ACK carry no TA and are attributed only to a channel, never a source (the addressing note in ctrl_frames.h) - so this detector is RTS-only.

Condition. `rule_rts_flood()` calls `ctrl_rts_flood(now, 60, rts.flood_rate, rts.flood_sustain_s, ...)` (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:462-463). `ctrl_rts_flood()` (src/ctrl_frames.c:278-305) walks sources whose `last_seen` is inside the trailing 60 s and returns the one with the largest RTS count that both (a) spanned at least `sustain_s` seconds (`dur = last_seen - first_seen >= 5` - timestamps refreshed by any TA-attributed control frame, not only RTS, floored at 1 s so a sub-second burst is not divided by zero) and (b) averaged `rate_per_s` (`n / dur >= 500`, integer division). `n` is the slot's cumulative RTS count and `dur` its whole lifetime since `first_seen` - neither is windowed to 60 s; the window only selects which sources are considered. The test is therefore a lifetime average: a source that floods for 10 s and then sends ordinary RTS keeps qualifying only while its average stays above 500/s, and a slot lives until evicted (`CTRL_MAX_SOURCES` = 64, LRU). The sustain requirement is the whole difference from a retry burst: "a client fighting interference emits RTS hard for a moment; a flood keeps going" (ctrl_frames.c comment). It writes back the source TA, the max NAV, and the duration.

Severity. `illegal_nav = max_nav > CTRL_NAV_MAX_LEGAL` (32767 us, the largest legal Duration per §9.2.4.2 - ctrl_frames.h:44-47). `mine = ownership_is_my_bssid(ta)`. CRIT when `illegal_nav || mine`, else WARN (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:484-486). Because `max_nav` includes PS-Poll AID values (above), `illegal_nav` is also true for any qualifying source that has ever sent a PS-Poll - i.e. a power-saving client - which is a benign route to CRIT; the rate and sustain tests still have to pass first. The detail reports the source, the RTS count, the duration, the computed per-second rate, the max NAV, and appends "above the legal maximum" and "YOUR network" as they apply. Key is `rtsflood:<ta>` (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:478). No evidence IDs, no match_ip, no pcap. Lifecycle: opens on the new key, holds while the source keeps qualifying each 60 s window, resolves 300 s after the last evaluation.

## The attack

802.11 arbitrates the shared medium with a Network Allocation Vector: every frame's Duration/ID field says how many microseconds the medium is reserved for the exchange that follows, and every radio in earshot sets its NAV accordingly and stays silent for that long - this is "virtual carrier sense," the mechanism that makes RTS/CTS work around hidden nodes (IEEE 802.11-2020 §10.3.2.4) [4]. An RTS (Request To Send) carries a large Duration because it is reserving the medium for the CTS, the data frame and the ACK that follow.

The attack, described by Bellardo and Savage at USENIX Security 2003 [3], floods the channel with RTS frames each carrying a large Duration. Every compliant radio in range defers for the reserved period, so the channel is silenced *without the attacker transmitting anything a frame-rate or deauth detector was watching for* - it is a denial of service built out of legitimate-looking control frames. The maximum legal NAV is 32767 us; the paper notes an attacker "need only transmit approximately 30 times a second to jam all access to the channel" in principle. Their crucial empirical finding, though, was that most commodity hardware did **not** properly reset its NAV from an oversized Duration - "most of the devices available to us do not properly implement the 802.11 MAC specification and are improperly resetting their NAV" - so the attack, while sound in principle, was "only a theoretical vulnerability due to implementation deficiencies in commodity 802.11 gear." That is why sloth checks the Duration against the legal maximum (a value no conforming radio emits, hence a generated frame) in addition to the rate, rather than trusting rate alone.

Tooling: there is no mainstream point-and-click RTS flooder; mdk4 offers only a CTS-creation modifier inside its packet fuzzer, not an RTS mode [5], so this is a scapy/custom-firmware technique (Bellardo and Savage built theirs on Intersil Prism cards - the Choice Microsystems MAC design - driven through the HostAP driver).

## Evidence that proves it

What sloth records. The alert detail carries the source TA, the RTS count over the window, the duration spanned, the per-second rate, and the max NAV, with the "above the legal maximum" and "YOUR network" flags. The `channel_summary` JSONL record carries per-channel `ctrl_total` / `ctrl_rts` / `ctrl_cts` / `ctrl_ack` / `ctrl_blockack` volumes (the measured half of channel occupancy, #64), and the `[m]` Channel view heat-colours the `ctrl` cell on the **RTS share** - a channel that is mostly RTS is one being reserved, the shape this alert fires on (docs/views/channel.md:108-116). No pcap is written (no match_ip, no evidence IDs).

What makes it real. One transmitter emitting >= 500 RTS/s sustained for >= 5 s is a rate a legitimate sender does not reach; that alone is the WARN. The decisive signal is `max_nav > 32767`: a Duration above the legal maximum cannot come from a conforming radio, so it is a generated frame, not a busy client - that is the CRIT and it is high-confidence. Corroboration: `RF_DEGRADED` on the same channel (retry/bad-FCS ratio climbing) is the observable a channel under reservation pressure produces.

What refutes it. A hidden-node pair on a genuinely congested channel can emit RTS hard - but not at 500/s sustained for 5 s with a legal-range Duration; the rate threshold is set well above saturation precisely to exclude this. A finding with a legal max_nav and a rate only just over threshold, from a normal client OUI, on a busy channel at a conference, is more likely heavy legitimate RTS/CTS than an attack. An illegal-NAV finding has one benign reading in this implementation: the source sent a PS-Poll, whose AID-carrying Duration/ID field (>= 49152) is folded into `max_nav`. Check whether the TA is a power-saving station before treating the CRIT as a generated frame.

## Benign causes

- **Hidden-node contention on a congested channel.** The one benign trigger the docs name: a pair of stations that cannot hear each other use RTS/CTS heavily. The rate threshold (500/s) and sustain requirement (5 s) are set well above what this produces, so it should not normally fire, but a pathologically congested channel is the case to rule out first.
- **Driver/firmware quirks.** A misbehaving radio could in principle emit RTS at an unusual rate; this would show a legal NAV and a single source, and is distinguishable from an attack by the absence of an oversized Duration.

- **PS-Poll from a power-saving station.** Not a radio quirk but a sloth artefact: the PS-Poll Duration/ID field carries the AID with bits 14-15 set, and `ctrl_observe_frame()` folds it into the same `max_nav` the CRIT test reads. A legitimate high-rate RTS sender that also uses legacy power save therefore reads as "above the legal maximum".

No conforming radio puts a Duration above 32767 in an RTS, so the CRIT branch has no benign explanation *for RTS frames*; the PS-Poll path above is the exception, and it is a sloth measurement defect rather than an ambiguity in the evidence. Recommendation: track `max_nav` from RTS frames only (or skip subtype 10).

## Assessment

Severity rationale. The two-tier split is well designed, with one implementation caveat: `max_nav` is taken over RTS, BAR, BA and PS-Poll frames, and PS-Poll's Duration/ID carries an AID >= 49152, so the illegal-NAV CRIT is not RTS-specific. A high sustained rate is suspicious but not conclusive (hidden nodes exist), so it is WARN; an above-legal Duration is a frame no conforming radio emits, so it is CRIT and genuinely high-confidence - this is the one signal in the family that distinguishes "generated" from "busy." Escalating a `--my-bssid` source to CRIT is also right.

Real-world prevalence. The NAV DoS is a foundational result [3] but the same paper showed its real-world efficacy is limited by the fact that most hardware mishandles oversized Durations, and there is no commodity tool that ships it [5]. So the *concept* is classic and the *practice* is rare - expect this alert to fire seldom, and when it does with an illegal NAV, to be meaningful.

Reference-host noise. **Not seen** - the reference host data for this type is null, meaning no RTS_FLOOD fired in the reference capture. That is exactly what the rule's high bar predicts in a quiet or normally-congested environment: 500/s sustained for 5 s from one TA is not reached by legitimate traffic. No dedupe or threshold change is indicated by the reference data. (If a specific deployment saw false WARNs from heavy legitimate RTS/CTS, the `rts.flood_rate` and `rts.flood_sustain_s` knobs are the intended adjustment, but nothing in the reference numbers calls for it.)

Is the evidence enough to act on? For an illegal-NAV CRIT, yes - that is a generated frame and a deliberate channel reservation. For a legal-NAV WARN, confirm the channel is not simply saturated with legitimate RTS/CTS (check `RF_DEGRADED` and the `channel_summary` RTS share) before treating it as an attack. First response: note the transmitter, the rate and the max NAV; check `RF_DEGRADED` on the same channel; there is no network-side control for another radio's RTS, so the only remediation is to locate the transmitter physically (docs/wiki/alert-remediation.md).
