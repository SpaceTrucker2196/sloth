## What the rule checks

Input is the probe-client table `s->probe_clients[]`, each row carrying a sliding-window flood detector `burst_win` fed by the monitor path. `probe_flood_note()` (`src/flood_window.c:55-69`) latches `flood = 1` when at least `k = probe.flood_frames` (default 30) probe-request timestamps fall inside a `PROBE_WIN_MS` = `probe.flood_window_s * 1000` (default 5000) half-open window; it records `burst_frames = k` and `burst_span_ms` (time between the first and last of those k). `probe_flood_refresh()` (:71-73) clears `flood` once `PROBE_HOLD_MS` = PROBE_FLOOD_HOLD_SECS 10 s passes with no new qualifying window.

`rule_probe_flood()` (`rules_wifi_mgmt.c:782-817`) re-checks the rate on the window's own evidence rather than trusting the flag: it skips a client whose `flood` is unset (:790), computes `win_rate = burst_frames / win_s` and requires `win_rate >= min_rate` where `min_rate = probe.flood_frames / probe.flood_window_s` (= 6/s at defaults, :786-787, :794-795), and requires `burst_span_ms < win_s*1000` (:796). It then fires `ALERT_TYPE_PROBE_FLOOD` at `ALERT_SEV_LOW` (:814-815). Key `probe_flood:<mac>` (:806), one incident per TA. Detail: `<mac> sent <frames> probes in <span>s (<rate>/s, >= <min_rate>/s over <win>s) - active recon / stuck client` (:807-811). No confidence, no evidence IDs, no `match_ip` - a plain `fire()` (:814), so no `--pcap-dir` carve. Because `burst_frames` is always set to `k = probe.flood_frames`, `win_rate` equals `min_rate` by construction and the rate test is a tautology at any setting; the live predicate is the window detector's trip plus the `burst_span_ms < window` check.

Durations run on the monotonic clock, so an NTP step neither creates nor breaks a flood (`docs/views/probe.md:78`). The rate test is deliberately a *rate*, not a total: before #88 the rule gated on lifetime `frame_count >= 30` and never compared the rate, so 30 probes over 300 s fired the same as 30 over 3 s; now 30 over 300 s does not fire and 30 over 3 s does. The window runs on one channel, so the 6/s is the sensor's share of a scan it is parked on (`docs/views/probe.md`).

## The attack

A probe request is how a client actively looks for networks: it transmits a management frame asking "is SSID X here?" (or a wildcard "any AP?") and listens for probe responses. Normal clients scan in bursts - a few requests per channel per scan, then a pause - so a monitor parked on one channel hears only a few per scan from any one device. A sustained >=6/s for 5 s from one transmitter address is outside that envelope. ATT&CK files active reconnaissance as T1595, "active scans ... where the adversary probes victim infrastructure via network traffic" [4]; the wireless equivalent is recon that touches the air, and sloth's corpus calls PROBE_FLOOD "the wireless equivalent: probe requests at a rate a client's own scanning does not reach" [1].

Three things produce the burst (rule header, :770-774): active recon tools walking channels or a target's PNL (hcxdumptool runs "layer 2 attacks against the WPA protocol", uses "three attack vectors" by default and may "only [be used] on networks that you have permission to attack" [6]; mdk4 mode p does "SSID Probing and Bruteforcing" - "Probes APs and checks for answer" [7]; ESP32 Marauder has a "probe request flood attack" [8]); a misbehaving or stuck client looping its own probes; and a KARMA-baiting attacker walking a PNL by probing each remembered entry to see which the surroundings answer for. What a scanner gains is a map of nearby SSIDs and clients; what a PNL-walker gains is the list of networks a device remembers - which is "gold for social engineering" and should be treated as sensitive (`docs/views/probe.md`).

## Evidence that proves it

sloth records the transmitter address, the number of probes, the span, and the computed rate in the detail. No frames are cited and no pcap is written (L2-only, no IP), so the detail *is* the evidence: a rate well above 6/s sustained across the window, from one address, on the channel the sensor is on. The probe view (`[3]`) and the PNL show which SSIDs that address asked for - a device probing a long list of specific named networks is PNL-walking or a tool; a device hammering a wildcard or one SSID is more likely stuck.

To call it recon rather than a glitch, look for persistence of the address over time and a spread of named-SSID probes (a tool walking a dictionary). To call it a stuck client, look for the same SSID repeated and the address persisting as a real (non-randomised) device. What refutes an attack reading: the address is randomised (modern phones), seen once, and the rate barely crosses - ordinary aggressive scanning by one device passing through. The remediation is explicit that "the address is as claimed and often randomised" and that a stuck client "produces the same burst", so the type is a lead, not a conviction (`alert-remediation.md:529`).

## Benign causes

- **A stuck or buggy client** looping probe requests - same burst shape, no attacker. The single most common benign cause; check whether the address persists as one real device.
- **Aggressive legitimate scanning** by a device searching hard for a network (roaming at a cell edge, a just-woken laptop).
- **IoT / cheap hardware** with poor scan backoff.
- The TA is **not identity**: "Starting in Android 8.0, Android devices use randomized MAC addresses when probing for new networks while not associated with a network" [9]; Apple devices use a per-network private address [10]; and it is trivially spoofed, so even the "who" is uncertain.

## Assessment

Severity LOW is correct - a probing radio is background recon, and the rule's own comment places the real risk in PNL leakage, not the probing itself. The #88 change to a true rate (rather than a lifetime total) is the right design and removes the main false-fire mode. Prevalence of aggressive probing is high, but the specific sustained-rate shape this rule gates on is tool/stuck-client behaviour, not ordinary scans.

The reference host confirms it is quiet and well-behaved: 3,067 fires on 7 keys, all LOW, ~110 s of held state per address - a handful of real bursts, not a firehose, and the per-TA key needs no extra throttle. A responder should treat a one-off LOW as noise, and only escalate when the same address recurs near the operator's own network - then correlate with `MY_NETWORK_RECON` and `RECURRING_TRANSIT` as the remediation directs (`alert-remediation.md:529`), remembering that a randomised address makes even the correlation only as good as the device fingerprinting behind it. No fix is needed for a one-off; the knobs exist (`probe.flood_frames`, `probe.flood_window_s`) to tighten or loosen the rate in a noisy band.
