## What the rule checks

Input: the per-BSS counter table in `src/fragattack.c`, fed by `frag_observe_frame()` on every data frame from the monitor thread (`src/capture/probe.c:535`) and read on main by `rule_fragattack()` through a locked row copy (`src/alerts/rules_twin.c:583-586`). The field is `eapol_relay` (`src/fragattack.h:226-232`; mirrored in `include/sloth.h:1464`).

The counter is incremented in `observe_locked()` (`src/fragattack.c:470`) when, for a data frame that is not Null/QoS-Null and not four-address (`:472`, `:477`, `:481`), all of (`:532-534`):

- **ToDS or FromDS is set** - an IBSS has no AP, so every ad-hoc EAPOL frame would otherwise match (`:527-531`);
- the **DA is unicast** - a group address can never equal the BSSID (`:529-531`);
- **neither SA nor DA equals the BSSID** - the BSSID, SA and DA roles follow the DS bits (`dot11_frame_decode`, per the table in `docs/wiki/fragattacks.md`): on a FromDS frame BSSID = addr2, DA = addr1, SA = addr3; on ToDS, BSSID = addr1, SA = addr2, DA = addr3;
- `first_frag_ethertype(f) == 0x888E` - fragment number 0, not an A-MSDU, body starts `AA AA 03` with EtherType EAPOL (`:158-176`).

Then `eapol_relay++` and `last_sa/last_da/last_hit` (`:535-538`). This runs **before** the key-install gate and regardless of the Protected bit (`:512-539` sits above `:550` and `:564`); in practice only a plaintext frame can satisfy the EtherType read, because on a CCMP/GCMP frame the bytes at the body start are the PN/KeyID octets, whose reserved byte is zero and never `0x03`. The detector therefore counts **plaintext unicast EAPOL frames whose addressing names two non-AP endpoints**, in either direction, with no handshake or association state consulted - the corpus and wiki call this the structural footprint and the reason this is the one plaintext rule in the family that needs no witnessed install (`docs/wiki/fragattacks.md:170-209`; [1]).

`rule_fragattack()` fires `ALERT_TYPE_FRAG_EAPOL_RELAY` at CRIT when `eapol_relay > 0` (`src/alerts/rules_twin.c:634-644`). Key `fragrelay:<BSSID>` (`:635`). Detail `"%u EAPOL frame%s forwarded between stations on %.17s - last %.17s -> %.17s, neither is the AP (CVE-2020-26139)"` (`:636-641`). `match_ip` NULL, `match_port` 0 (`:642-643`): no per-alert pcap (`src/alert_pcap.c:105`). Resolution is 300 s after the last re-assert (`include/sloth.h:860`), which only happens when the 64-row BSS table evicts the row (`src/fragattack.c:47-48`; `bss_get()` refreshes `last_seen` on every data frame on that BSS, `:220-228`) or the process restarts; the reference host's two `reopens` are consistent with that. `last_sa`/`last_da` are one pair per BSS row shared by every FragAttacks branch.

Two notes on scope. First, `src/fragattack.h:108-123` still says this CVE "stays out"; that comment predates the code at `src/fragattack.c:512-539` and should be updated. Second, the rule compares against the BSSID only; it has no notion of an AP MLD, which is the gap #180 reports (see Benign causes).

## The mechanism

"Devices must accept plaintext 4-way handshake frames when a client is connecting to a network. We found that some devices also forward plaintext handshake frames if they are destined to other clients in the network, even when the sender has not yet authenticated (CVE-2020-26139). Affected devices are FreeBSD and NetBSD APs, and certain home routers such as our Asus RT-N10 and Linksys WAG320N" [2, Section 6.6]. The CVE record: "An Access Point (AP) forwards EAPOL frames to other clients even though the sender has not yet successfully authenticated to the AP. This might be abused in projected Wi-Fi networks to launch denial-of-service attacks against connected clients and makes it easier to exploit other vulnerabilities in connected clients" [3].

Why it matters to the rest of the family: "An adversary can abuse this to perform the A-MSDU attack from Section 3 using only a multi-channel MitM position. In particular, the adversary first associates with the target network. Then, instead of starting the 4-way handshake, the adversary will send a handshake, i.e., EAPOL, frame to the AP with as final destination a client that is connected to the network. A vulnerable AP accepts and forwards this EAPOL frame to its destination, in this case the targeted client. Moreover, the AP will encrypt this frame towards the client. The adversary can then use its MitM position to set the A-MSDU flag in the encrypted EAPOL frame" and inject an arbitrary packet via the second subframe [2, Section 6.6]. Against NetBSD a large EAPOL frame also makes the AP "fragment, encrypt, and forward it", a way to manufacture encrypted fragments for the cache and mixed-key attacks [2, Section 6.6].

What is on the air, and what sloth sees of it. The adversary's uplink EAPOL is a plaintext ToDS data frame with addr1 = BSSID, addr2 = the adversary (an associated but unauthenticated station), addr3 = DA = the victim client: SA and DA are both non-AP, so the rule fires on that frame. The AP's forwarded copy toward a *connected* client is encrypted [2, Section 6.6], so its EtherType is unreadable and sloth does not count it; toward a client that is itself unauthenticated (the DoS variant) it would be plaintext FromDS with addr3 = SA = the adversary and would also count. So the alert proves "a station transmitted a unicast EAPOL frame addressed to another station through the AP" - the attempt - and says nothing about whether the AP forwarded it, which is the receiver-side bug the CVE is about. Tooling: `eapol-inject <MAC>` ("Test if AP forwards EAPOL frames before authenticated (use tcpdump)") and `eapol-inject-large` [5].

## Evidence that proves it

What sloth records: `alert`/`alert.create` JSONL under `fragrelay:<BSSID>` with the detail (frame count, BSSID, `last SA -> DA`); the sloth.db `alerts` row for the key with `first_seen`, `last_seen`, `count` (= evaluations) and `reopens`; the `[c]` view row with `eapol_relay` and the pair (`docs/views/fragattack.md`). The EAPOL collector (`--collect-handshakes`) keys on `(BSSID, STA)` pairs and may or may not hold the frame. No per-alert pcap.

What makes it real: a plaintext unicast data frame with EtherType 0x888E whose SA and DA are two **station** addresses that have both been seen as clients of that BSSID (the `assocs` table / `assoc` records), with the SA's station having associated but **not** completed a 4-way handshake (no M3/M4 for `(BSSID, SA)` in the `eapol` records); and, confirming the AP is vulnerable, a subsequent encrypted frame from the AP to the DA of EAPOL-sized length shortly after [2, Section 6.6]. The `eapol-inject` test's own verification is "use tcpdump" on the destination [5].

What refutes it (the benign fingerprint): the non-BSSID address is the **AP itself under another name** - specifically, on a Wi-Fi 7 BSS, the BSSID with the U/L bit set and the last octet off by one, or the MLD MAC the Basic Multi-Link element in the beacon carries; the frames are M1/M3 (AP to station) or M2/M4 (station to AP) of an ordinary handshake for a station that then associates and passes traffic normally; the other "station" is the AP MLD [11]. That is exactly the reference-host incident below.

## Benign causes

- **802.11be AP MLD addressing.** On a Wi-Fi 7 multi-link AP the authenticator's address in EAPOL is the AP MLD MAC, while addr2/addr1 on the air is the per-link BSSID; the two differ by design, so every AP-originated EAPOL on an MLO link fails the `mac_eq(sa, bssid)` test (`src/fragattack.c:533`) and the station's replies fail the `mac_eq(da, bssid)` test. Open issue #180 reports this from the reference host: SA `3a:60:41:b4:24:2f` against BSSID `18:60:41:b4:24:30`, "the BSSID with the U/L bit set and the last octet decremented by one", on an Arcadyan AP with `phy = Wi-Fi 7` and eight associated stations [11]. This is the dominant false positive and is widening as Wi-Fi 7 ships.
- **Any topology where the authenticator's MAC is not the link BSSID.** The rule tests addresses only (`:532-534`), so it inherits every such case; MLO is the one documented. A controller-terminated 802.1X or a vendor that stamps a management MAC into EAPOL would present the same shape; none has been observed on the reference host, and this is stated from the code, not a source.
- **Group-addressed EAPOL (EAPOL-Start / Logoff to the PAE group address 01:80:C2:00:00:03)** is excluded by the unicast test (`:532`); **IBSS** by the DS-bit test; **WDS / mesh** by the four-address skip (`:481`). These are design exclusions, not residual risks.

## Assessment

The premise is sound for every AP before 802.11be: EAPOL is a station-to-authenticator protocol, and a unicast plaintext EAPOL frame addressed from one station to another through the AP has no legitimate reading on an infrastructure BSS, so CRIT on the first frame was a defensible choice and needs no key-install gate (`docs/wiki/fragattacks.md:170-209`). The premise fails on an AP MLD, where "the AP" has two addresses and the rule knows one. #180's fix - accept the BSSID, the MLD MAC `mle_observe()` stored for that link, or, on an 11be BSS only, the U/L-flipped sibling address, and mark the detail when the heuristic cleared it - restores the rule's footing; until it lands, a `fragrelay` incident on a Wi-Fi 7 BSS should be read as probably benign and one on an older AP as probably real. The count-semantics defect is the family's: one frame, one permanent CRIT, millions of evaluations.

Prevalence: an AP-side implementation flaw found on BSD APs and some home routers in 2021 [2, Section 6.6]; CVSS 5.3 NVD, 5.7 Cisco [3][6]; patched across vendors (62 advisory links [9]); no in-the-wild use reported [4][7]. A real attacker needs to be associated to the target network (open, or holding the PSK) and to want to reach a specific client; it is a stepping stone for the A-MSDU attack, not an end in itself.

Reference host: **seen** - 1 row, 1 key, max severity CRIT, 2 reopens, first_seen 2026-10-31, last_seen 2026-11-01, 2,964,009 fires. Against the count semantics: at the 4 ticks/s floor 2.96 M evaluations would take 8.6 days, longer than the two-day window, so the cadence was well above the floor - #180 puts the standing condition at about 42 hours, which is roughly 20 evaluations a second, in line with the event-wake behaviour the FRAG_* siblings measured on this host. The detail's leading integer was 2 frames when #180 captured it; `reopens` = 2 matches its `incidents = 3`. #180 identifies the key as the Arcadyan Wi-Fi 7 AP and the SA as the MLD-derived sibling of the BSSID, with the DA a client that associated to that BSSID: the likely reading is the AP's own M1/M3 to its own client, not a relay. That is a likely reading, not a fact - nothing in sloth's record shows the Basic MLE's MLD MAC for that BSS (`ecs.mld_mac = null` in the bundle, which #180 itself flags as needing to be resolved first), so the identification rests on the address derivation and the phy label. #180 is open; the fix is in progress in `src/fragattack.c`.

Sufficient to act on alone? No. A responder should (1) read the BSSID's phy from the `[w]`/AP views or the `beacon_ap` record: if it is Wi-Fi 7 and the SA or DA is the BSSID with the U/L bit set and the last octet within one, treat it as #180's false positive and triage it `false`; (2) otherwise check whether SA and DA are both known clients of that BSSID (`assocs`) and whether `(BSSID, SA)` has a completed handshake in the `eapol` records - an associated station with no M3/M4 sending EAPOL to another client is the attack shape; (3) capture the channel externally and watch for the AP's encrypted forward to the DA [2, Section 6.6][5]; (4) if confirmed, the AP is the vulnerable device: patch it against the vendor advisory [9] per `docs/wiki/alert-remediation.md:1187-1201`, and look for the follow-on `FRAG_AMSDU` on the same BSS. No tuning knob exists; do not suppress the rule on Wi-Fi 7 BSSes by hand, fix the identity test (#180).
