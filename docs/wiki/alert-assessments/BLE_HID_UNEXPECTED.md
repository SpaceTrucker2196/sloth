## What the rule checks

Input is `s->ble_devices[]` (MAX_BLE_DEVICES 256, include/sloth.h:2676), one row per (sensor kind, iface, address, address type), with `hid` bits - `BLE_HID_APPEARANCE` (0x01) when the Appearance AD is 0x03C0..0x03CA, `BLE_HID_SERVICE` (0x02) when the 16-bit service list carries 0x1812 - `addr_type`, `first_seen`, `last_seen`, `appearance` (include/sloth.h:2787-2788; src/ble_detect.c:12-18). `ble_appearance_is_hid()` encodes Bluetooth SIG category 0x00F (Human Interface Device, subcategories 0x01 Keyboard .. 0x0A Presentation Remote; appearance = category << 6 | sub) [6][7].

**Form (a), `hid_oui_findings()`** (src/ble_detect.c:116-148): a row with `hid != 0` and a **public** address type (0 or 2) whose first three octets are in `kHak5Ouis[]` (00:13:37 Hak5, 00:C0:CA Alfa) or `kEspressifOuis[]` (eleven ESP32/ESP8266 blocks, including AC:0B:FB, the ESP32-S2 used by the Flipper Wi-Fi devboard, flagged "unverified" in the source) (src/wifi_oui_attacker.c:8-35). Key: `blehid` + `oui` + iface + MAC (lines 141-146). Detail (src/alerts/rules_ble.c:100-113): `public address <mac> (<Hak5|Espressif> OUI table) advertises a HID appearance[ and ][the HID service 0x1812] via <iface> - a keyboard-class advertiser on attacker-helper silicon; precursor shape of CVE-2023-45866, no pairing observed`.

**Form (b), `hid_rotate_findings()`** (src/ble_detect.c:172-219): per sensor iface, only once that sensor's first advertisement is older than the window (`sensor_first_ts`, lines 186-187 - so the first 900 s of a run, when every address is new, cannot qualify), count rows with a **random** address type (1 or 3), `BLE_HID_APPEARANCE` set, and `first_seen > now - ble.hid_rotate_window_s`; fire when `count >= ble.hid_rotate_addrs` (lines 175-177, 190-199). Key: `blehid` + `rotate` + iface (lines 213-216). Detail (src/alerts/rules_ble.c:115-124): `<n> new random addresses advertised a HID appearance (last 0x<appearance>) via <iface> in <W>s - one keyboard-class advertiser rotating its address, or several new ones; precursor shape of CVE-2023-45866, no pairing observed`.

Both WARN, `match_ip` NULL (no pcap). A known limit (src/ble_detect.h:72-76): a row evicted from the 256-row table and heard again gets a fresh `first_seen`, so on a busy site a resident random-address keyboard can count as "new"; `evict_ble_device` in sensor_health says when that is happening. `--ble-rotation-hints` deliberately does not merge addresses for this rule, because hint tokens are attacker-supplied (src/ble_detect.h:78-82) [8].

## The attack

CVE-2023-45866, published 2023-12-08 (CWE-287, CVSS 3.1 6.3 AV:A/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N): "Bluetooth HID Hosts in BlueZ may permit an unauthenticated Peripheral role HID Device to initiate and establish an encrypted connection, and accept HID keyboard reports, potentially permitting injection of HID messages when no user interaction has occurred in the Central role to authorize such access" [3]. The researcher, Marc Newlin of SkySafe, describes the family as "tricking the Bluetooth host state-machine into pairing with a fake keyboard without user-confirmation", after which the attacker types into the victim [4]. The proof of concept pairs an emulated keyboard against Android 4.2.2-14 (11-14 fixed at the 2023-12-05 patch level, 4.2.2-10 "will not be patched"), Linux with the default configuration of BlueZ 5, macOS 12-14 (14 fixed in 14.2; 12 and 13 "with no patch expected") and iOS 16-17 (17 fixed in 17.2; 16 "with no patch expected"); it presents over **Bluetooth Classic** with Class of Device 0x002540 (keyboard), from an Ubuntu 22.04 host with a Broadcom adapter [5]. Apple's HT214035 (iOS 17.2, 2023-12-11) credits the same researcher: "An attacker in a privileged network position may be able to inject keystrokes by spoofing a keyboard" [9].

What an attacker gains is a keyboard on the victim's unlocked device: open a terminal, install software, exfiltrate - the Hardware Additions technique T1200, "a keyboard that should not be there" (include/sloth_alerts.def:308) [1][10]. The hardware is a laptop, a Raspberry Pi, or the sort of ESP32-based or Hak5 hardware whose OUIs form (a) reads [1].

Why sloth reads advertisements for it: an LE HID peripheral advertises its class in the Appearance AD (Bluetooth SIG category 0x00F) and/or lists the HID service UUID 0x1812 [6][7]. A keyboard-class advertiser on attacker-helper silicon (public address, Hak5/Espressif OUI), or several keyboard-class advertisers appearing on fresh random addresses in a short window, is the **advertising shape** of a device positioning itself to be accepted. The repo is explicit: the CVE is the host accepting the peripheral; sloth sees no pairing, no connection and no HID report, never connects or reads GATT, and the public PoC is BR/EDR, so the rule corroborates and never confirms [1][2].

## Evidence that proves it

What sloth records:

- form (a) detail: the public address, which OUI table, whether appearance and/or 0x1812 was advertised, the sensor; form (b) detail: the count of new random addresses, the last appearance value, the window, the sensor (src/alerts/rules_ble.c:100-124);
- `ble_alert` JSONL, `kind: hid_oui` (`addr`, `addr_type`, `hid`, `appearance`, `oui_table`) or `hid_rotate` (`addrs`, `window_s`, `appearance`), change-only on `addrs` by powers of two (docs/wiki/jsonl-schema.md:732);
- the `ble_device` records for the address(es): every decoded field - address type, appearance, service UUIDs, local name if sent, RSSI, first/last seen, sensor - also in the `[*]` view's Enter detail (docs/wiki/cli-reference.md:373);
- sensor_health `evict_ble_device` (table churn that can inflate form (b)), `btcap_sampling`;
- **no pcap**.

To call form (a) real: a `ble_device` row with a public address in a Hak5 or Espressif block [11], Appearance 0x03C1 (Keyboard) or 0x03C0 (generic HID) [6], and/or 0x1812 in the service list [7], that is **not** a known product of the operator's; ideally a BR/EDR LAP from the same hardware (the `--bt-capture` Ubertooth stream keeps LAPs) and, if any host in the room later shows a new paired keyboard it did not ask for, that is the exploit - which sloth cannot see. To call form (b) real: three or more random-address rows with a HID appearance, each first seen inside the last 15 minutes, with near-identical RSSI (one transmitter) and the same appearance value; a resolvable private address rotating on the spec's recommended 15-minute interval [8] would give exactly one new address per window, so three in a window from one device means a rotation faster than the spec recommends or several devices.

What refutes it: form (a) with a local name that matches a consumer keyboard or remote (Espressif sells into exactly that market [1]); form (b) where the addresses are spread in RSSI and persist for days (several real keyboards arriving); `evict_ble_device` rising at the same time (a resident keyboard being re-created with a fresh `first_seen`, src/ble_detect.h:72-76); a `sampled` Ubertooth source where the "new" addresses are simply the first time that channel heard a long-present device.

## Benign causes

- **Legitimate ESP32-based peripherals.** Keyboards, remotes, game controllers and DIY macro pads use Espressif silicon with public addresses; the repo calls a public Espressif address advertising HID "unusual, not hostile" [1].
- **A room filling with people carrying BLE keyboards/mice** at the start of a meeting - form (b) needs only three new random HID advertisers in 15 minutes.
- **A retail display or IT rollout** of new BLE keyboards powered on together.
- **A keyboard that rotates its private address faster than the 15-minute recommendation** - permitted by the spec (RPA_Timeout may be as low as 1 s) [8].
- **Device-table churn** on a busy site re-creating a resident keyboard's row (src/ble_detect.h:72-76).
- **Hak5 hardware is also sold to legitimate pentesters**; a Pineapple on an authorised engagement is a true positive the operator may already know about.

## Assessment

**Severity.** WARN is right, and arguably generous: both forms are a precursor shape and the public exploit uses the other transport [1][5]. The rule is honest about that in the detail ("no pairing observed"). Anything higher would need a pairing or connection observation sloth does not make.

**Prevalence.** The vulnerability was universal across the four major stacks until December 2023 and is patched everywhere maintained [3][4][5][9]; unpatched Android and Linux devices remain. No public count of real-world keystroke-injection attacks was found.

**Noise on the reference host.** Not seen (host null). When it fires: form (a) is one incident per address, `observations` 1 - quiet. Form (b) is one incident per sensor whose detail changes as addresses enter and leave the 900 s window - a few observations per incident, and the `ble_alert` record quantises `addrs` by powers of two. `count` climbs at 4/s while the form holds, as for every rule; show `observations`. If form (b) fires in a venue with many keyboards, raise `ble.hid_rotate_addrs` (2..256) before shortening the window.

**Sufficient to act on?** As a prompt to look at the device, yes; as evidence of an attack, no - and the detail says so. The decisive evidence is on the potential victim hosts (an unexpected paired keyboard, `bluetoothctl paired-devices`), not on the air.

**First response.** Note the address and OUI table (form a) or the count and appearance (form b); look for a legitimate keyboard or remote; check that hosts in range carry the CVE-2023-45866 fixes (BlueZ patched, Android 2023-12-05 or later, macOS 14.2, iOS 17.2) (docs/wiki/alert-remediation.md:1780) [3][5][9].
