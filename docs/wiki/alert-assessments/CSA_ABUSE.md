## What the rule checks

Inputs. The CSA event ring `s->csa_events[]` (`SLOTH_CSA_MAX_EVENTS` = 128, include/sloth.h:2416, 3510), filled by `csa_observe()` (src/action_snoop.c:451). Two producers feed it: a Spectrum Management action frame (category 0, action 4 - src/action_snoop.h:26,42) carrying a Channel Switch Announcement element (tag 37) or Extended CSA element (tag 60), decoded in `csa_frame()` (src/action_snoop.c:484-519); and a beacon whose IE set carried either element, passed in from src/capture/probe.c:556 as `CSA_SRC_BEACON`. Each event stores addr3 (`bssid`, the BSS it claims to speak for) and addr2 (`ta`, the real transmitter) separately - "a forged one is where they differ, and that is the whole signal" (probe.c comment) - plus `new_channel`, `new_op_class`, `switch_mode`, `switch_count`, `source`, `from_channel` and `ts`. A `new_channel` of 0 or above 233 is dropped at record time as malformed (action_snoop.c:459).

Condition. `rule_csa_abuse()` makes one pass per BSSID that has an event inside `CSA_STORM_WIN_SECS` (60 s), deduping BSSIDs it already handled this call (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:566-573). `csa_snapshot()` copies the ring newest-first (src/action_snoop.c:555-568), so the event a BSSID's pass is built from is its **newest** in-window event: `spoofed`, `to_twin`, `to_downgrade` and the channel/op_class/mode/source in the detail are all read from that one event, and only `storm` looks across every event for the BSSID. For that BSSID it computes three shape signals (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:576-596):

- **spoofed** - `source == CSA_SRC_ACTION && memcmp(ta, bssid) != 0`. Only action frames are tested; a beacon's addr2/addr3 are equal by construction, so a mismatch there means the frame was misread, not forged (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:578-579). Consequences of testing the newest event only: a forged action-frame CSA stops tripping `spoofed` as soon as a newer CSA for the same BSSID arrives - e.g. the real AP's own beacons carrying a CSA during a genuine DFS move, which repeat every beacon interval - and a forged *beacon* CSA never trips it at all.
- **storm** - `csa_distinct_targets(bssid, now, 60) >= CSA_STORM_THRESH` (4). `csa_distinct_targets()` counts distinct `new_channel` values for that BSSID in the window (src/action_snoop.c:522-535). A legitimate AP picks one target and commits.
- **to_twin / to_downgrade** - scans the beacon inventory for an AP already sitting on the announced `new_channel` that is the rogue half of an evil-twin episode (`btm_candidate_is_twin`), or that advertises a weaker security lane (`downgrade_flags`, composing with #62) (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:588-596).

The rule fires when `spoofed || storm || to_twin` is true (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:598); `to_downgrade` is not a trigger but does raise severity. Key is `csa:<bssid>` (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:607). Severity is CRIT when `spoofed || to_twin || to_downgrade || mine`, else WARN (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:623-624). The detail names the target channel, op_class, switch mode, source (action/beacon), and appends the forged TA, a storm note, "target hosts a known twin", and "YOUR network" as they apply; a pure storm also appends the distinct-target count and window (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:608-620).

Lifecycle. fire() opens the incident on the new key; it stays open while any CSA for the BSSID keeps re-qualifying, and `expire_incidents()` resolves it `ALERT_RESOLVE_AFTER_S` (300 s) after the last evaluation (include/sloth.h:860, engine.c:919). No frame-level evidence IDs and no `match_ip` are passed (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:622-625), so this alert writes no pcap.

## The attack

A Channel Switch Announcement is the 802.11h mechanism by which an AP tells its associated clients "I am moving to channel N in `switch_count` beacon intervals" (IEEE 802.11-2020 §11.8 / §9.6.2.4) [3][4]. It exists for a legitimate reason: Dynamic Frequency Selection requires an AP that detects radar on a 5 GHz DFS channel to vacate within 10 seconds, and a graceful move takes the clients along rather than dropping them [5]. The CSA can ride a beacon, a probe response, or a unicast Spectrum Management action frame; `hostapd` exposes it as the `chan_switch` CLI command [10].

The abuse, first described by Konings et al. in 2009 [6], is that the announcement is unauthenticated on a network without management-frame protection: an attacker forges a CSA in the AP's name and moves the victim's client to a channel of the attacker's choosing. Two payoffs follow. First, denial of service - a client steered to an empty or illegal channel loses its AP, and it is cheaper and quieter than a deauth flood and works on firmware that ignores deauth, because roaming/CSA support is a certification checkbox. Second, and more important, it is the standard way to establish the multi-channel machine-in-the-middle position: Vanhoef's channel-based MitM clones the AP on a different channel - KRACK describes it as "a channel-based MitM attack, where the AP is cloned on a different channel" [7] - and FragAttacks states that position was "reliably obtained ... by spoofing channel switch announcements" [8]; both attacks rest on it. A forged TA is the unambiguous tell, because in a genuine action-frame announcement addr2 equals addr3.

Tooling: the channel-based MitM scripts shipped with the KRACK and FragAttacks research use spoofed CSAs [7][8]; the commodity flooder mdk4 has no dedicated CSA mode [9], so this is a crafted-frame technique (scapy, custom hostapd, the research scripts) rather than a point-and-click one.

## Evidence that proves it

What sloth records. The alert detail carries the BSSID (addr3), the announced channel and op_class, the switch mode and source, and - when present - the forged transmitter address (addr2), a storm flag with the distinct-target count, and the twin/own-network context. The underlying `wifi_csa_event` JSONL record (src/jsonl.c, keyed `bssid`+`ts`) is emitted for every CSA sloth hears, legitimate DFS included, and keeps `ta` as the real transmitter separate from `bssid`, plus `new_channel`, `new_op_class`, `switch_mode`, `switch_count`, `source` (0 beacon / 1 probe-resp / 2 action) and `from_channel` - so a consumer sees the ordinary announcements around the flagged one. No pcap is written (no match_ip, no evidence IDs).

What makes it real. A forged-TA finding is the strongest (but its absence proves little, since the test only reads each BSSID's newest event - check the `wifi_csa_event` stream for any action-frame record with `ta` != `bssid`): an action-frame CSA whose addr2 differs from the addr3 it claims to speak for cannot come from the real AP, so `spoofed` alone is high-confidence. A storm - four or more distinct target channels from one BSSID in 60 s - is behaviour no conforming AP produces, since the real AP picks one channel. The corroborating shape is a same-SSID AP that actually appears on the announced channel right after (the landing site); where that AP is already an evil-twin episode half, the steering intent is explicit.

What refutes it. A single CSA from the real AP (addr2 == addr3) announcing one channel, followed by that AP actually beaconing on the new channel, is a legitimate move - DFS or auto-channel. The `switch_count` counting down across successive beacons and `from_channel` matching where the AP was are the fingerprint of an orderly, genuine switch. A WARN that is only a `to_downgrade` CRIT escalation (no forged TA, no twin) is weak evidence of attack: it is flagging the neighbourhood, not the announcement.

## Benign causes

- **DFS radar events.** An AP on a 5 GHz DFS channel (52-64, 100-144) that detects radar must leave within 10 s [5]; a well-behaved one sends a CSA to carry its clients along. This is the single most common benign trigger and is noted in the docs/views/alerts.md row.
- **Auto-channel assignment.** Controller-driven channel optimization (Cisco DCA and equivalents) moves APs off congested channels and announces the move with a CSA. Rapid oscillation under interference can approach the storm threshold.
- **Legitimate steering.** 802.11v/k deployments move clients for load balancing; a CSA is one tool among several. These are genuine addr2 == addr3 announcements and never trip the forged-TA branch.
- **Multi-VAP and mesh.** A radio advertising several BSSIDs, or a mesh node re-homing, can emit CSAs that, on a hopping sensor with a partial inventory, look like they target channels sloth has not yet mapped.

Telling them apart: the forged-TA branch has no benign reading; the storm branch has a weak one (a flapping AP); the twin/downgrade branches are only as trustworthy as the evil-twin detector and the neighbourhood's security posture.

## Assessment

Severity rationale. Forged TA and a twin-hosting target earning CRIT is correct - both are specific to abuse. The storm branch at WARN is a reasonable "behaviour no AP should show." The weak point is `to_downgrade`: it raises any firing to CRIT because some AP on the target channel offers a transition-mode (WPA2/WPA3) or mixed lane, which is extremely common and says nothing about this announcement. That branch inflates severity on the basis of the neighbourhood rather than the frame.

Real-world prevalence. CSA abuse is a documented primitive with a 2009 origin [6] and is the enabling step for the best-known modern Wi-Fi attacks [7][8], but there is no public telemetry on how often it is seen in the wild, and it is not in the commodity flood toolkits [9]. Treat it as a targeted, low-base-rate finding.

Reference-host noise. 27,571 fires on 10 keys at max_sev CRIT. Because `count` is per-tick (<= 4/s) and each announcement holds its key for up to 60 s, 2,757 ticks/key is on the order of tens of announcements per BSSID over the capture - quiet, not noisy. Cadence caveat: the tick arithmetic assumes the default 250 ms poll with an idle keyboard. `poll_tick()` runs once per main-loop iteration (`poll_data()` at src/main.c:1628 calls it at :205) and the wait before it is cut short by any keypress or by `event_wake_signal()` on a new or escalated alert (src/alerts/engine.c:526, 608), so real ticks/s is >= 4 and every duration derived here is an upper bound; `--refresh-ms` changes the base. The per-BSSID key already dedupes correctly; no throttle is needed. The thing worth doing is grading, not volume: that ten distinct BSSIDs each reached CRIT, with no CSA flood mode in common tools, strongly suggests most are `to_downgrade`/twin escalations rather than forgeries. A responder should read the detail suffix first.

Is the evidence enough to act on? For a forged-TA or storm finding, yes - that is a crafted frame or impossible behaviour. For a bare `to_downgrade` CRIT, no - corroborate with the `wifi_csa_event` stream (is addr2 == addr3? did `switch_count` count down? did the AP actually move?) before treating it as an attack. First response: do not join or probe the target channel; note the BSSID, announced channel and whether the TA was forged; check which clients followed; if the transmitter is not the operator's and is on premises they control, locate and remove it, else report it to whoever runs the site (docs/wiki/alert-remediation.md).
