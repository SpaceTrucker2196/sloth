## What the rule checks

Inert until `wifi_baseline_ready()` (src/alerts/rules_regulatory.c:222): before the session's RF baseline freezes every AP would read as "new," so the rule does nothing. Then, over `s->beacon_aps` (lines 224-225), for each AP:

- `ap_band_label(a->first_seen_channel, a->channel_source) == "5 GHz"` — uses `first_seen_channel` (set on insert, never updated, include/sloth.h:1585), so a later move does not rewrite where the AP started; channel 0 (never set) reads as "?" and is excluded by this test (lines 226-231).
- `a->frame_count >= DFS_MASK_MIN_BEACONS` (3) — one misread DS Parameter Set cannot place it on a DFS channel (line 232, include/sloth.h:2498).
- `!wifi_baseline_has_bssid(a->bssid)` — the BSSID is new since the baseline froze (line 233).
- `reg_channel_is_dfs(cc, ch, &which, &cac_s)` — the first-seen channel is a DFS channel in the compiled-in regulatory table (src/reg_table.c): keyed by the AP's own Country element where it names a jurisdiction the table has a source for (US->FCC, EU member states->ETSI, src/reg_table.c:136-140), and by the union of every table otherwise (lines 164-175). The DFS set is 52-64 and 100-144 (src/reg_table.c:40-101) (lines 235-238).
- `!csa_led_to(s, a, ch)` — no Channel Switch Announcement leads there (lines 240, helper 168-185).

`csa_led_to()` accepts a CSA in the ring (SLOTH_CSA_MAX_EVENTS 128) naming `ch`, sent by the BSS it names (`addr2 == addr3`, i.e. `e->ta == e->bssid`), heard no later than DFS_MASK_CSA_SLACK_S (2 s) after the AP's first beacon, and either in the AP's own BSSID, or from a BSSID beaconing the same non-empty SSID that has *since been heard on `ch` itself*. The last condition defeats the #63 CSA lure: a CSA forged in the real AP's name (addr3 is spoofable) naming a DFS channel, then a same-SSID twin coming up there; the forger cannot make the real AP actually move, so while the real AP keeps beaconing on its old channel the peer CSA does not excuse the newcomer (comment lines 136-167).

Severity WARN if `ssid_impostor` (designated SSID from an undesignated BSSID) or `twin` (already the twin half of an evil-twin episode — never the `real_bssid` half, which the twin rules judged legitimate and which stays LOW, `bssid_is_twin_half()` lines 37-43), else LOW (lines 242-245, 268-270). Key: `dfsmask:<bssid>` (lines 250-254). Detail (lines 257-267): BSSID, SSID, DFS channel, the table name and its as-of date, the CAC seconds, "heuristic, no public advisory," and the corroboration/ownership tails. Technique deliberately empty (a shape, not an observed adversary action); pinned by `tests/test_research_corpus.c :: test_uncitable_kinds_with_a_real_basis_are_still_cited`.

## The attack

On the DFS channels — 5.25-5.35 GHz (channels 52-64) and 5.47-5.725 GHz (100-144) — radar has priority, so a transmitter must perform a Channel Availability Check: listen silently for radar before it may transmit, 60 s normally [3], extended to 10 minutes on the weather-radar channels 120/124/128 (whose bandwidth falls in 5600-5650 MHz) under ETSI [4]. 47 CFR 15.407(h)(2): "U-NII devices operating with any part of its 26 dB emission bandwidth in the 5.25-5.35 GHz and 5.47-5.725 GHz bands shall employ a DFS radar detection mechanism"; (h)(2)(ii): "may start using the channel if no radar signal ... is detected within 60 seconds" [3]. ETSI EN 301 893 V2.1.1 §4.2.6 and Table D.1 give the same 60 s CAC, with "For channels whose nominal bandwidth falls completely or partly within the band 5 600 MHz to 5 650 MHz, the Channel Availability Check Time shall be 10 minutes" [4].

Consequently a BSS normally reaches a DFS channel in one of two ways: it was there before the monitor started (so it is in the baseline), or it moved there by announcing a Channel Switch (so there is a CSA leading there) [1][5]. A BSSID that appears for the first time, cold, on a DFS channel *after* the RF picture settled, with no CSA, is the shape the #101 issue names as a WIDS-evasion: an evil twin stood up on hostapd with `ieee80211h=0` (DFS off), on a channel most clients scan only passively and many WIDS cover lightly, hiding where defenders look least [1]. No public advisory names "DFS-masking" as an evasion pattern, which is why the rule assigns no ATT&CK ID and stays a heuristic [1].

## Evidence that proves it

The detail (above) names the BSSID, SSID, DFS channel, the regulatory table used and its as-of date, the CAC duration, and any corroboration. No per-alert pcap: `fire(...)` is called with `match_ip = NULL`, `match_port = 0` (line 268) — the evidence is the beacon stream and the CSA ring (L2). The substrate is the `beacon_ap_t` row (`first_seen_channel`, `frame_count`), the `src/wifi_baseline.c` membership, and the `s->csa_events` ring; all are visible in the beacons view and the CSA/beacon detail screens. JSONL `alert.*` records carry `key`, `detail`, `sev`, no `technique` (empty, dropped), and the observations/evaluations split — `observations` (~1) is the event tally, not `count` (src/jsonl.c:574-575).

To read it as the evasion, the reader wants: a BSSID not in the baseline, first heard on a 52-64 or 100-144 channel, with >=3 beacons, and no CSA with `addr2 == addr3` naming that channel in the ring — ideally with corroboration (a designated SSID from an undesignated BSSID, or the BSSID already a twin half). What refutes the evasion reading: a legitimate AP known to use that DFS channel that just rebooted (it would reappear cold after its CAC), a --hop run that reached the channel late (the AP is old but the sensor's first sighting is late), or a genuine CSA that the rule did see (then it is suppressed).

## Benign causes

The dominant benign cause is a legitimate AP rebooting onto a DFS channel after completing its CAC: observationally identical to the evasion, explicitly called out by the repo corpus and the #101 issue, and the reason the rule is LOW [1]; the regulatory texts [3][4] only establish that such an AP must complete a CAC first. A second is a channel-hopping receiver whose first sighting of a quiet, pre-existing DFS AP lands after the baseline froze (the AP is not new, but reads as new) — the rule's stated known limit (comment lines 216-220). A third would be any AP that changed channel to a DFS channel without a CSA the sensor caught (a CSA pushed out of the 128-entry ring, or a move the hopping radio missed). The discriminator for WARN is outside corroboration only; on its own a DFS_MASKING row is a "new DFS arrival, worth a glance" note, not an incident.

## Assessment

Severity LOW/WARN is well-judged: the raw observation is unambiguous but its interpretation is not, so the rule reports the fact at LOW and promotes only on independent corroboration (SSID impostor or twin-pair membership). The empty ATT&CK technique is correct — the regulatory clauses are a real citable basis, but "appeared on a DFS channel" is a shape, not an adversary action. On the reference host it did not fire (host data null), and it is structurally quiet: gated on a frozen baseline, restricted to new BSSIDs on a small DFS channel set, needing 3 beacons. No threshold tuning is warranted; if a dense environment with churn made it chatty, the levers are the baseline settle behaviour and filtering LOW rows in the view. The evidence is enough to prompt a look, not to conclude an attack: a responder confirms whether the BSSID is known operator/vendor gear that uses that DFS channel (benign reboot) before treating a WARN-level DFS_MASKING — a designated SSID from an unconfirmed BSSID, or a twin-pair half — as a rogue AP to locate and report, and should correlate with CSA_ABUSE and the evil-twin family [remediation: docs/wiki/alert-remediation.md].
