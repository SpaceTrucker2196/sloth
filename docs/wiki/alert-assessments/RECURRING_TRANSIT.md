## What the rule checks

Inputs: the transit table `s->transits[]` (MAX_TRANSIT_DEVICES = 64 rows, include/sloth.h:1881), filled not from probes directly but from presence classification. Each poll, `st_transit()` (src/poll.c:222-240, WITH_PCAP builds only) walks `s->probe_clients[]`, and for every client currently classified `PRESENCE_TRANSIENT`/`passing` by `presence_classify()` (a device whose RSSI rose into a peak and fell back out within a short dwell; src/presence.c, defaults PRESENCE_SWING_DBM = 8 and PRESENCE_TRANSIENT_MAX_S = 120 in src/presence.h:47,54, tunable as `presence.swing_dbm` / `presence.transient_max_s`) it calls `transit_observe(id, c->last_seen, c->signal_dbm)` (poll.c:237 — the timestamp is the probe row's `last_seen`, not the wall clock, so re-feeding the same row on later ticks never manufactures a pass) with a canonical identity from `transit_canonical_mac()` (MLO assertion first, then the numerically smaller seqnum-correlated sibling, one hop only; transit.c:111-147) so a randomizing device's rotated MACs count as one.

A precondition that shapes what "a pass" can be: `presence_classify()` returns TRANSIENT only when the probe row's dwell (`last_seen - first_seen`) is <= 120 s and the RSSI ring has the rise-peak-fall shape (presence.c:81-83). The probe table drops a client after PROBE_AGE_SECS = 120 s of silence (include/sloth.h:1806, src/capture/probe.c:945). So each recorded pass needs the device to have gone quiet for > 120 s first (row aged out, `first_seen` reset) and then to have been audible for <= 120 s with a passing RSSI shape. A device that stays audible for more than two minutes on a visit is never "passing" on that visit and contributes no pass, which is why a resident or a parked car does not trip the rule.

`transit_observe()` (transit.c:28-69) records a "pass": it updates best_rssi (closest approach) and `last_seen` on every call, and appends the timestamp to `pass_ts[]` (capped at MAX_TRANSIT_PASSES = 16, oldest dropped) only if the gap since the last recorded pass is >= `transit.gap_secs` (300 s). This coalescing is the point — a slow drive-by that produces a run of transient verdicts is one pass, not several (transit.c:49-56).

Condition (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:981-1006): for each transit row, count passes whose timestamp is within `transit.window_secs` (7200 s) of now; fire if that count >= `transit.recur_thresh` (3).

Key (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:998): `transit:<canonical mac>`. One incident per canonical device.

Detail (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:999-1003): `<mac> passed <n> times in <m>m (closest <d>dBm) - circling`. Carries the canonical MAC, pass count, span in minutes, and closest approach in dBm. Two quirks: `<n>` counts only passes inside the window, but `<m>` is `last_seen - pass_ts[0]`, measured from the oldest *retained* pass (up to 16 kept), which can be older than the window — so "passed 3 times in 300m" is possible with a 120-minute window; and `best_rssi` is the strongest `signal_dbm` fed since the transit row was created (never reset while the row lives), not only within the windowed passes. It does not carry the individual pass timestamps (those are only in the in-process transit table, which nothing exports — see Evidence) or any SSID.

Severity WARN (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:1004-1005). Plain `fire()` — no confidence term, no match_ip, no evidence frames. The rule stops re-asserting as soon as fewer than `transit.recur_thresh` passes fall inside the window, and the transit table separately drops a device whose `last_seen` (any transit observation, coalesced or not — not only a recorded pass) is older than `transit.window_secs` (transit.c:85-99, the age test at :95); the incident resolves 300 s after the last re-assertion.

## The attack

One device driving past is traffic. The same device driving past three times in two hours is a circuit — and it is an observation an operator cannot make unaided, because nobody remembers which of two hundred MACs they saw forty minutes ago (the rule's own comment, rules_wifi_mgmt.c:970-975; docs/personas/wifi-surveyor.md:215-223). A surveillance vehicle casing a site, a wardriver mapping a neighbourhood on repeated circuits, or a persistent follower leaves exactly this signature: the same radio, at the same place, on separate visits.

MITRE frames reconnaissance that touches the target as T1595 Active Scanning; research/mitre/T1595.md records RECURRING_TRANSIT as sloth's own extension of it to the physical layer — "a device seen repeatedly over separate visits ... the same radio passing the same place, which is a pattern only a persistent sensor can see" [1][3]. The identity problem is the same one that governs all passive device tracking: a modern phone randomizes the address it probes with [13][14] (the iOS 14+ / Android 10+ defaults in [9][10] are the per-network *association* address, a separate mechanism), so recognizing "the same device" across passes depends on correlating its addresses. sloth does this by MLO assertion and seqnum correlation (transit.c:111-147), which is the technique Vanhoef et al. (AsiaCCS 2016: as much as 50% of devices tracked for at least 20 minutes) and Martin et al. (PETS 2017: ~96% of tested Android phones) showed defeats randomization for a large fraction of devices [13][14] — but only as well as the correlation holds. The surveillance threat this serves is the Snoopy model of profiling people by the Wi-Fi their devices emit [11] (the 2013 UK "smart trash cans" that "used Wi-Fi to track the movements of people" were the same mechanism, per [13] §1 citing Ars Technica [12]).

Crucially, the same signature is produced by a delivery van on a round or a neighbour with a short commute (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:977-980). The rule reports the circuit and leaves intent to the operator.

## Evidence that proves it

What sloth records: the detail line's canonical MAC, pass count, span and closest approach. No pcap (no match_ip), no cited frames. The browsable corroboration is the `[p] Probe` view (presence classification and RSSI trajectory of the underlying client) and `[j]` seqnum correlations (what addresses were folded into this canonical identity). The transit table itself holds the individual `pass_ts[]` timestamps, but nothing exports it: no JSONL record, no SQLite table and no view reads `s->transits` (its only readers are `src/poll.c` and this rule), so the pass timestamps and the transit `best_rssi` exist only in the running process. The durable record is the alert itself — the JSONL `alert` stream and the SQLite `alerts` row — which is what the transit.c:88-90 comment means by "the durable record is the SQLite sink". (jsonl.c:1994 does emit a `best_rssi`, but it is `wifi_merged_t`'s strongest-RSSI-across-radios field in the multi-sensor merge record, include/sloth.h:2650, not the transit table's closest approach.)

To call it a real recon lead: look at the pass pattern and closest approach. Passes clustered at regular intervals with a consistently strong closest-approach (the vehicle stops or slows at the same spot) is more interesting than scattered weak passes. Cross-check `MY_NETWORK_RECON`: a device that both recurs physically and probes the operator's designated SSID without associating is a far stronger lead than either alone (docs/wiki/alert-remediation.md explicitly pairs them). Check `[j]` to confirm the canonical identity is sound — a genuine single device whose addresses correlated cleanly, not two unrelated MACs merged.

What refutes it (benign fingerprint): regular, clock-like recurrence matching a delivery or commute schedule (same times each day), a device that also associates somewhere nearby (a resident, not a passer), or passes whose closest approach is always weak and transient (genuinely driving through, not casing). If the canonical identity is actually several distinct devices mis-merged by a loose seqnum correlation, the pass count is an artifact — `[j]` is the check.

## Benign causes

- **Delivery rounds.** A courier passing a site several times a day is the textbook false positive, named in the rule comment and research/mitre/T1595.md [1].
- **Commuting neighbours / colleagues.** A short commute past the sensor twice a day, over a window, trips three passes.
- **Scheduled transit.** A bus or service vehicle on a route carrying an AP or a passenger's phone.
- **Correlation artifacts.** A loose seqnum/MLO merge can combine unrelated devices into one canonical identity, manufacturing recurrence; a randomizing device whose addresses did NOT correlate is split into several identities, each possibly below threshold (the opposite failure).

## Assessment

Severity rationale: WARN is correct and the rule is honest — it reports a pattern ("circling") and the detail carries the pass count and closest approach so the operator can judge. It never asserts intent, and the docs and code both foreground the benign causes. CRIT would be wrong because the passive signal cannot separate a surveillance circuit from a commute.

Real-world prevalence: physical reconnaissance by repeated passes is a real activity (wardriving circuits, casing a target) and the observation is genuinely one only a persistent sensor can make, which is the rule's reason to exist. But it is rare relative to benign recurrence, and passively indistinguishable from it — so a firing is far more often a neighbour than a threat (research/mitre/T1595.md is explicit [1]).

Reference-host noise verdict: 21,074,626 fires on 16 keys. The rule counts per tick while a transit row stays above the 3-passes-in-2h bar, so ~1.3M ticks per key ≈ at most 91 hours of standing condition each (ticks run at 4/s or more) — meaning these 16 canonical identities kept re-crossing the threshold across many separate 2-hour windows over the whole capture, not that 21M events occurred. Sixteen keys is a small, hand-triageable set; the inflation is entirely in the per-tick `count`. The pass-level coalescing (TRANSIT_GAP_SECS) already prevents a single slow drive-by from manufacturing recurrence, and canonicalization already folds a randomizing device's addresses. Recommendation: surface the pass count from the detail instead of `count`, and not `observations`. Between passes the detail is mostly constant - the minutes term is `(last_seen - pass_ts[0]) / 60`, and `last_seen` moves only while the device is being observed passing (transit.c:45, fed from the probe row's `last_seen` at poll.c:237) - but `passes` also drops as old passes leave the 7200 s window (rules_wifi_mgmt.c:992-996), and every detail change (pass count, minute boundary, `best_rssi`) makes `observations` rise on every tick until the next 60 s update refreshes the stored `detail_sig` (engine.c:487, 534), so each change costs about 60 s worth of ticks. No threshold change is warranted; 16 keys is the right order of magnitude for a sensor watching a road.

Is the evidence sufficient to act? For a single firing, no — it is a lead by design; the fix from sloth's side is none, the operator supplies the context (docs/wiki/alert-remediation.md "RECURRING_TRANSIT"). A responder should note the device and its passes, remember that MAC randomization means the identity is only as good as the correlation behind it (check `[j]`), and treat it as actionable only when it correlates with something else — `MY_NETWORK_RECON` on the same identity, a consistently strong closest approach, or a pass schedule that matches no plausible benign routine.
